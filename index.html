How these tests are kept fair

    Weeks 3–6 temperature outlook experimental

    Evidence: Experimental a live statistical correction of CFSv2, tested leave-one-year-out (2019–2026); no frozen forward test yet.

    How likely is each week to be warmer or colder than normal? "Normal" is the 1991–2020 climate of that place and time of year (CPC station analysis): a week counts as warmer than normal if its mean temperature is in the warmest 40 % of past weeks, colder if in the coldest 40 %, so with no forecast information both chances would be 40 %.

    Method: the 16 runs of NOAA's CFSv2 seasonal model started on the date shown (4 runs at each of 00/06/12/18 UTC) are statistically corrected with a probabilistic bias correction in the spirit of Microsoft's PBC (Guan et al. 2026): for each 1° grid cell and week, the observed outcome is regressed on the ensemble-mean forecast over seven years of past CFSv2 forecasts (2019–2026) from the same time of year.

    Skill is modest, as for all forecasts this far ahead: in leave-one-year-out cross-validation over 902 past forecasts the ranked probability skill score against climatology is (0 = no better than climatology, 1 = perfect). Part of the skill comes from the warming trend since 1991–2020. Rainfall at these lead times showed almost no skill, so it is not shown.

    The models

    Three kinds of forecast are shown. All of them start from the 00 UTC time of the day shown at the top.
    AI · run here

    AI weather foundation models, trained on decades of past weather (mostly the ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis). We run them ourselves every day on NVIDIA L40S GPUs, all started from the same NOAA GFS analysis, so the starting weather is shared; differences still mix the model itself with how its inputs are converted, its grid and time step, and how its output is processed. Each takes minutes rather than hours.

    Physics · run here

    Numerical models that solve the equations of the atmosphere on a grid. We run them ourselves every day on CPU nodes, also started from the GFS analysis. They range from a 12 km regional weather model (WRF) to global climate models (CESM, E3SM) run in forecast mode.

    Downloaded

    The official forecasts of national weather centres (NOAA, ECMWF, Environment Canada, DWD, Met Office, Météo-France, NASA, US Navy), downloaded each morning from their open-data services. Each starts from that centre's own data assimilation, which is usually the biggest advantage over the models we run.

    Methods and caveats

    • Next 48 h (3-hourly) shows the first two days at 3-hour steps from the models that output that often (for example ECMWF IFS, NOAA GFS, Pangu-Weather's 3-hour model, WRF, CESM, E3SM and ICON, as available that day). Next 48 h (hourly) shows the models with hourly output. Map times are UTC with US Central time in brackets; precipitation is the 3 h (or 1 h) total ending at that time.
    • Temperatures are in °F, precipitation in inches, wind in mph. City charts are shown in each city's own local time (daylight saving included).
    • Verification: every model's forecasts from the last seven 00 UTC cycles (re-run as hindcasts where the daily run did not exist yet) are interpolated to ~2,400 ASOS/AWOS weather stations (Iowa Environmental Mesonet) and compared with the hourly reports at 00/06/12/18 UTC. Temperature, humidity and wind speed are ranked by root-mean-square error (RMSE, lower is better); the index of agreement (IOA, Willmott 1981: 1 − Σ(F−O)² / Σ(|F−Ō|+|O−Ō|)², 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than the observed mean) is shown for every variable except wind direction; wind direction by mean absolute angle error (MAE, only when the observed wind is ≥5 kt); bias is forecast minus observed. Precipitation compares 24 h totals at ASOS stations and ~19,000 CoCoRaHS volunteer gauges (24 h ending at the morning reading, matched to 12 UTC), and is ranked by the equitable threat score for ≥0.1 in (ETS, higher is better; 0 = no skill); ETS for ≥0.5 in (heavier rain) is also shown. All ranked models are scored on exactly the same station times. Stations measure one point while models predict a grid-box average; temperature is moved from each model's terrain height to the station's height (6.5 °C per km), but wind and humidity are not adjusted, so coarse models are still penalized over mountains. The formula of every score is under Scores and formulas.
    • Fairness. Every ranking puts the models on exactly the same station times (or grid cells), and the tags under each model name show what still differs. Made equal: the same cases and stations for every ranked model; temperature moved to station height; the long-term tables start the AI models we re-ran from the ECMWF IFS analysis, a real-time start, and show runs started from ERA5 (a reanalysis made days later) as an unranked reference; rain is also checked against ASOS gauge reports (rainfallAI was fitted to the MRMS gridded analysis, not to these station reports; MRMS itself uses gauge data, so this is an additional observation-based check, not proof of complete independence; other models' training data are not audited here); our rainfallAI models were frozen on 8 November 2026, and the clean forward test scores every model from the 9 November cycle on. Not equal, keep in mind: (1) rainfallAI's versions were chosen after looking at scores from the 1 July – 20 October 2026 test period, so its numbers for that period are optimistic (tag "version chosen using this period"); (2) rainfallAI, rainfallAI-T, rainfallAI-W, the National Blend of Models and our statistical baseline are post-processed: trained or calibrated on observations, while the other rows are raw model output, so each row also gets a rank within its group; rainfallAI also takes other models' forecasts (GFS, GEFS, NBM, HRRR, ECMWF) and observations at the start time as inputs, so it is a product built on them; (3) ensemble means are smoother than single runs, which lowers RMSE but blurs heavy rain, and member counts differ (tag "ens. mean N"); single members are not stored, so they cannot be scored separately; (4) in the daily forecasts and the weekly and forward tables, the models we run start from the NOAA GFS analysis while downloaded forecasts (ECMWF IFS, AIFS and the others) start from their centre's own analysis, usually an advantage; (5) the physics models we run (WRF, MPAS, ICON, CESM, E3SM) use our setup, with GFS starts and no data assimilation of their own, so their scores mean "as run here", not the best their developers can do; (6) coarse models (1° and 1.5° grids, tag "N° grid") lose points when a grid-box average is compared with a point station; (7) most AI models have no 2 m humidity, so theirs is estimated from the lowest pressure level and they are listed apart from the humidity ranking; (8) the MRMS radar grid and the URMA analysis are what rainfallAI was trained against, so the rain-gauge and station scores are its fairer tests; (9) rainfallAI's rain amounts follow a rule tuned to the threat score, which a wet bias can raise, so read the threat score together with the rain-day bias; (10) some developers fine-tuned their models on ECMWF analyses (operational GraphCast, AIFS, Aurora 1.5); we run every model as released and fine-tune none; (11) GenCast outputs every 12 hours, so it is ranked only in the 00 and 12 UTC view, and daily highs and lows come from 6-hourly output; (12) forecasts become available at different times (GFS about 03:30 UTC, ECMWF about 07:00 UTC, ours later), and the scores compare equal start times, not equal delivery times; ECMWF open data are at 0.25°, coarser than the model itself.
    • Short-range models (HRRR, NAM, RRFS, ARPEGE) stop at their last forecast hour. Models covering less than 97% of the stations are listed below the ranking with their own-domain score, so they do not change the other models' station sample.
    • "Day N" means hours 24(N−1)–24N after the start time. Maxima and minima use 6-hourly output, so true daily extremes may be slightly higher or lower.
    • Spread is the standard deviation across all models that predict a field: it measures how much the models disagree. It is not a calibrated forecast uncertainty: models share errors, so they can agree and still be wrong.
    • Most AI models do not predict surface humidity or rain. For them, surface humidity is estimated from the model's lowest pressure level above ground (1000/925/850/700 hPa), so humidity-based indices are approximate. Fields a model cannot provide are left out.
    • Indices: heat index (NWS Rothfusz); WBGT (simplified, shade); Fosberg Fire Weather Index; Hot-Dry-Windy Index (surface approximation: VPD × wind); reference ET from Hargreaves–Samani; 7-day P − ET₀ as the forecast's contribution to drying or wetting. Drought indices such as SPI or the US Drought Monitor need months of observations and are not forecast here.
    • The climate models (CESM, E3SM) cycle their land surface from day to day. CESM's land has come from a spin-up since the 31 Oct 2026 run; earlier runs had a cold-started land with a cold bias of about 8 °F, so its scores count only runs from 31 Oct on, and it is listed as not ranked until it has enough forecasts. E3SM's land starts from a 1-year spin-up from 30 Oct 2026, and its scores likewise count only runs from that date.
    • Data: NOAA/NCEP products are public domain. ECMWF IFS, AIFS and ensemble forecasts © ECMWF, licensed under CC BY 4.0 (ECMWF open data). Other centres' data are used under their open-data terms. Several AI model weights are licensed for non-commercial use only.
    • Research product. Use official NWS forecasts for decisions.

    Scores and formulas

    How every number in the accuracy tables is computed. FFF is the forecast, OOO the observation, iii runs over the cases a table adds up (station times, or grid cells × start dates) and wiw_iwi​ is the weight of case iii. Arrows in the table headers show which direction is better.

    Every score below is built from weighted means or weighted counts like this one.

    wi=1w_i=1wi​=1

    Every report counts once, so regions with many stations (and many CoCoRaHS volunteers) count for more than sparse ones.

    wi=cos⁡φiw_i=\cos\varphi_iwi​=cosφi​

    Each grid cell is weighted by its area (φ\varphiφ = latitude of the cell centre), so every part of the contiguous US counts by its size: a cell at 25°N counts about 1.4 times one at 49°N. Area weighting since 9 November 2026; before that every cell counted once.

    Adding up lead days

    Sˉ=17∑d=17Sd\bar S=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{d=1}^{7}S_dSˉ=71​d=1∑7​Sd​

    Long-term rain tables: a score SdS_dSd​ is pooled over all cells (or gauges) and starts for each lead day ddd, then averaged over days 1–7.     Amounts: temperature, humidity, wind and rain totals

    Bias 0 is best

    Bias=⟨F−O⟩\mathrm{Bias}=\langle F-O\rangleBias=⟨F−O⟩

    Positive: the forecast is too high (too warm, too humid, too windy, too wet).

    Mean absolute error lower is better

    MAE=⟨ ∣F−O∣ ⟩\mathrm{MAE}=\big\langle\,|F-O|\,\big\rangleMAE=⟨∣F−O∣⟩

    The typical size of the error, in the variable's units.

    Root-mean-square error lower is better

    RMSE=⟨(F−O)2⟩\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\big\langle (F-O)^2\big\rangle}RMSE=⟨(F−O)2⟩​

    Like MAE, but large errors count more. Ranks temperature, humidity and wind speed. Smooth forecasts such as ensemble means tend to score well on it.

    Index of agreement higher is better

    IOA=1−∑iwi (Fi−Oi)2∑iwi(∣Fi−Oˉ∣+∣Oi−Oˉ∣)2Oˉ=⟨O⟩\begin{gathered}\mathrm{IOA}=1-\frac{\sum_i w_i\,(F_i-O_i)^2}{\sum_i w_i\big(|F_i-\bar O|+|O_i-\bar O|\big)^2}\\[4pt] \bar O=\langle O\rangle\end{gathered}IOA=1−∑i​wi​(∣Fi​−Oˉ∣+∣Oi​−Oˉ∣)2∑i​wi​(Fi​−Oi​)2​Oˉ=⟨O⟩​

    Willmott (1981). 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than forecasting the observed mean everywhere.

    Correlation higher is better

    r=⟨(F−Fˉ)(O−Oˉ)⟩⟨(F−Fˉ)2⟩ ⟨(O−Oˉ)2⟩r=\frac{\big\langle (F-\bar F)(O-\bar O)\big\rangle}{\sqrt{\big\langle (F-\bar F)^2\big\rangle\,\big\langle (O-\bar O)^2\big\rangle}}r=⟨(F−Fˉ)2⟩⟨(O−Oˉ)2⟩​⟨(F−Fˉ)(O−Oˉ)⟩​

    Does the forecast put more rain where more fell? Ignores the overall amount (4-week table).

    Skill against climatology higher is better

    MSESS=1−⟨(F−O)2⟩⟨(C−O)2⟩\mathrm{MSESS}=1-\frac{\big\langle (F-O)^2\big\rangle}{\big\langle (C-O)^2\big\rangle}MSESS=1−⟨(C−O)2⟩⟨(F−O)2⟩​
